There is a moment in every project where the work stops being about discovery
and starts being about arithmetic. The interesting decisions are behind you.
What is left is the accounting: the fourteen small wrongnesses you have been
politely ignoring, each of which will take an afternoon.

Most people call this the boring part. I have come to think it is the only
part that reliably improves the thing.

<aside class="pullquote">
The last ten percent is not the tax you pay on the work. It is the work.
</aside>

## The arithmetic is the craft

A friend who restores furniture told me that the difference between a
competent chair and a good one is not in the joinery — anyone can be taught
joinery — but in the eighty minutes of sanding after you are already tired of
the chair. He said it without any drama. It was a fact about chairs.

I think about that more than I think about most advice, because it is
unfalsifiable in exactly the right way. You cannot see the eighty minutes. You
can only see the chair.

> Everything that is worth doing has a long, dull middle distance where the
> only motivation available is the one you brought with you.

The temptation, always, is to start something else. A new project is pure
possibility; an unfinished one is a list of known faults. That asymmetry is
the whole trap, and knowing it is a trap does not help very much.

## What actually helps

Three things, none of them clever:

- **Make the list finite.** Write down every remaining wrongness, including
  the embarrassing ones. Fourteen is a number. "Nearly done" is not.
- **Do the worst one first**, while you still have the patience for it. The
  order is not neutral.
- **Stop calling it polish.** Polish sounds optional. Say *finishing*, which
  is a trade, and has been for six hundred years.

None of this makes the last stretch enjoyable. It makes it legible, which
turns out to be almost as useful.

## A caveat, honestly held

I am suspicious of any essay that ends with a tidy method, my own included.
Some things should be abandoned. The skill is not finishing everything — it
is knowing which unfinished thing is unfinished because it is hard, and which
is unfinished because it was never any good.

I have not found a reliable test for that. I suspect there isn't one, and that
the best anyone can do is wait a week and look again with a cold eye.
