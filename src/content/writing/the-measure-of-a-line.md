Ask a typographer how long a line of text should be and you will get a number
with a shrug attached. Sixty-five characters. Sixty-six. Forty-five to
seventy-five, says Bringhurst, which is the kind of range that sounds like
precision and functions like permission.

The shrug is the honest part. The number is a summary of something the eye
does automatically and the reader never notices: the return sweep, the small
mechanical act of getting from the end of one line to the start of the next
without losing your place.

<aside class="pullquote">
A measure is not a width. It is a promise that you will find your way back.
</aside>

## Why long lines fail

Past about eighty characters, the sweep gets unreliable. You land a line low,
re-read a phrase you have already read, and the text starts to feel heavy for
reasons you would not be able to name if asked. It is not that long lines are
tiring to read. It is that they are tiring to *navigate*, and navigation is
invisible until it fails.

Short lines fail differently. Under about forty characters the sweep happens
so often it becomes the dominant rhythm, and prose starts to read like a
poem that has not earned it.

> The eye is a poor ruler and an excellent judge. It cannot tell you the
> measure. It can tell you instantly when the measure is wrong.

## The part nobody puts in the style guide

Measure is not independent of everything else. A generous line height buys you
a few more characters, because the return sweep has more vertical room to
land in. A larger size on a screen held at arm's length behaves like a smaller
size held close. A serif with a strong horizontal axis carries the eye further
than a geometric sans.

So the number moves. What does not move is the test, which takes four seconds:
set a paragraph, read it at a normal distance, and notice whether you have to
*work* to get from line to line. If you do, the measure is wrong, and no
amount of argument about Bringhurst will fix it.

This site is set at roughly sixty-five characters, because I ran the test and
that is where it landed. That is the entire justification. I think it is
enough of one.
